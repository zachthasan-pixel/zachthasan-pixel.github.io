Is It Time to End the United Nations?

Power, Legitimacy, and the Future of Collective Security

In one week of September 2026 the General Assembly heard the president of a permanent member weigh aloud whether to annihilate another member's government, and, two days later, a prime minister wanted by the International Criminal Court. Seven months earlier the Security Council had condemned Iran's retaliation for the strikes that began the war, and never once voted on the strikes themselves.

Is it time to end the United Nations? Zahir Hasan asks the question in earnest. He follows the idea of world organization from the Concert of Europe to the League of Nations' last Assembly in Geneva, shows how the veto was priced and paid for at Yalta and San Francisco, and reads the record of the last decade, Gaza, Ukraine, Sudan, the Iran war, the money and the machine, against the condition the founders themselves wrote down in 1945: that an organization unable to keep the peace, or able to keep it only at the expense of law and justice, should expect to be dissolved.

His answer is not yet, and not like that. The book proposes a Second Charter, drafted by a General Conference under Article 109 of the Charter, the one route to reform that the veto cannot block, with a fixed date for the five permanent members to accept it or be left behind, and a calendar that runs from 2027 to 2031. It tests the design against the vetoes of the last decade, and the model texts, including a complete draft resolution and draft treaty articles, are in the appendices.

Fifteen chapters, four appendices, more than 570 sources, research current to 26 September 2026.
