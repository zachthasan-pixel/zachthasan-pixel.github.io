AI and the Law

AI is not a legal person. When an AI system causes harm, the law asks what the people and organizations around it did — who built it, who sold it, who deployed it, who relied on its output, and who had a duty to check.

AI and the Law turns that principle into practice. Written for students, educators, professionals, creators, business owners, and anyone who now works alongside these systems, it covers copyright and ownership, training data, plagiarism and disclosure, deepfakes and digital replicas, defamation, privacy, facial recognition, surveillance, emotion recognition, algorithmic hiring, automated decisions, liability, consumer protection, education, healthcare, autonomous vehicles, finance, AI agents, cybersecurity, legal practice, and the protection of children and vulnerable users.

It compares four systems in depth — the European Union, the United States, Canada, and Japan — and explains how an organization operating across all four builds one control system rather than four policies.

Every chapter separates four kinds of statement, so a reader can always tell settled law from agency guidance, from a genuinely open question, from practice. Every chapter closes with the same seven questions applied to its own subject. Every chapter carries worked examples with real numbers.

The second half is a working toolkit: ten model documents including an impact assessment template, a vendor due diligence questionnaire, an organizational AI use policy, a classroom AI statement, a contract schedule, and an incident response playbook. Six applied case studies follow real decisions from problem to resolution. A 27-question FAQ, a 59-entry glossary, and a full index make it usable as a reference.
