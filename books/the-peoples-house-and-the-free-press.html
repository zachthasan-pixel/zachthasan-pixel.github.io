The People’s House and the Free Press

Trump, White House Access, and the First Amendment

A president may condemn journalism as fiercely as he likes. That is politics. When the White House switches off a credential because it dislikes the coverage, the question stops being political and becomes constitutional.

On a Friday afternoon in September 2026, the President of the United States announced that CNN, MS NOW and Politico were banned from the White House. The next morning their correspondents arrived at the gate and found that their passes no longer worked.

Zahir Hasan follows that question from Robert Sherrill's refused pass in 1977, through Jim Acosta, Brian Karem and Simon Ateba, to the Associated Press and its fight over two words on a map — an appeal the D.C. Circuit had still not decided when this book closed.

He walks the White House room by room, from the briefing room to Air Force One, and shows why the constitutional answer changes as you move. He traces the quarrel back through Nixon, Kennedy, Roosevelt, Wilson, Lincoln and the Sedition Act of 1798, and he is as unsparing about the Obama administration's treatment of Fox News as about the present one.

The government's strongest case is set out in full before it is answered. The verdict that follows is calibrated rather than shouted: no newsroom is owed a seat at every table, but criticism cannot lawfully become the price of admission.

A rule that protects only the press you happen to like is not a rule at all.

An evidence-led account for lawyers, journalists, students of American government, and anyone who wants to know what the First Amendment actually requires when a president closes a gate.
