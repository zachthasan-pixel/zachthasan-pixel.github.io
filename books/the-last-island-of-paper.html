The Last Island of Paper

The Eccentric Economists, Stubborn Artisans, and Quiet Revolutionaries Fighting the Death of Physical Currency

Money used to have a body. Somebody is trying to keep it alive.

In a windowless fortress in France, a sixty-four-year-old engraver carves lines into steel that no scanner can copy. In a basement laboratory, a neuroeconomist pays volunteers to feel the small, measurable sting of handing over cash. In Stockholm, a retired policeman fights a country that abolished money without ever voting on it. In Zurich, a man in a cardigan keeps a fortune in thousand-franc notes because he is afraid of a Tuesday. And in a central bank, a mild public servant shows the author the future of money: a box on a screen where you can type a rule.

The Last Island of Paper is a journey through the strange, stubborn world of the people who still know what money feels like—engravers, campaigners, tollbooth operators, family-office managers, financial-crime investigators, and the designers of programmable currency—and a report from the places where the paper has already gone: the Swedish supermarket that will not take kronor, the Indian queues of November 2016, the Spanish town where the lights went out and only a biscuit tin of notes still worked.

It is a book about convenience, and what convenience costs. It is a book about the last room in the economy with no camera in it.
