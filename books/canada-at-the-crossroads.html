Canada at the Crossroads

Multiculturalism, Allegiance, and the Meaning of Citizenship

Can a country be generous without being naive?

Canada's official multiculturalism policy has always invited a hard question: can a country protect cultural heritage without weakening the shared civic life that makes it governable? For decades the comfortable answer was yes. That answer no longer comes easily.

Drawing on Statistics Canada data, the findings of the Hogue Commission on foreign interference, current polling on Canadian public opinion, and a wide body of research on immigrant integration, Quebec nationalism, and Indigenous rights, Canada at the Crossroads argues that the country's diversity remains workable — but only if paired with a renewed civic compact: real credential recognition for skilled immigrants, transparent public spending, immigration levels matched to housing and service capacity, and a security posture that protects targeted communities rather than treating them as suspects.

Neither a celebration of multiculturalism nor an obituary for it, this book takes seriously both the strongest case for the policy and the strongest case against it — then asks what Canada must actually do, starting now, to keep its bargain with itself.

Written by a Canadian-born public policy, law, and education scholar, Canada at the Crossroads includes a glossary, a timeline of Canadian multiculturalism policy since Confederation, discussion questions for book clubs and classrooms, and 34 fully cited sources with URLs and DOIs.
