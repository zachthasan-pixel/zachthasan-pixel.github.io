The Permanent Suspect

Islamophobia's Long Run Through North American Life

A caricature does not survive contact with a person.

For more than two decades, North American news, film, and policy have taught

millions of people to recognize Muslims as a threat before recognizing them as

neighbors. The Permanent Suspect traces how that habit of mind was built —

and how it endures.

The story begins before September 11, 2001, in the older Orientalist imagery

that primed audiences to see the Muslim world as backward and threatening. It

follows the post-9/11 security state as it hardened that imagery into policy:

watchlists, secondary screening, surveillance programs, and travel bans that

fell disproportionately on Muslim citizens and immigrants. It tracks how

Hollywood convention, and later streaming-era algorithms, kept the caricature

in circulation long after the headlines moved on. And it follows the same

pattern north of the border, through Quebec's Bill 21, the Quebec City mosque

shooting, and Canada's own security and immigration apparatus.

Grounded in court filings, government reports, peer-reviewed research, and

named, dated events rather than appeals to feeling alone,The Permanent

Suspect insists throughout on a rigorous, checkable standard of evidence.

It does not ask readers to take fear or grievance on faith — it shows its

sources, and lets the record speak.

The book argues for a simple, exacting standard: that belonging in a

democratic society is not a feeling extended by the goodwill of a majority,

but a public practice, sustained or withheld by specific, nameable

institutional choices — in newsrooms, in writers' rooms, in legislatures,

and in security agencies.

Written for readers of history, media criticism, and civil rights — and for

anyone who has ever been asked to prove, once again, that they belong —

The Permanent Suspect is a documented account of how a community was made

permanently suspect, and what it would take to undo it.
